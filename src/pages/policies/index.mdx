# Policies and support

These rules exist because of what this course grades: a genuine
investigation trail, not a correct-looking final state. Each policy below
follows from that, rather than restating generic university rules that
already apply regardless of this course.

## Investigation integrity

The graded artefact is the investigation you actually carried out —
observations, hypotheses, disconfirming tests, evidence, diagnosis,
intervention, and verification, in the order you did them. Locating a fault
through undirected trial-and-error and then writing up a hypothesis and
evidence sequence that was never actually used to find it is a misrepresentation
of the work, not a shortcut to the same mark. Assessments 2–4 use lightweight
checkpoints and a running log for exactly this reason: not to audit every
keystroke, but to fix an honest intermediate record of the investigation
against which the final submission can be read.

## Collaboration

All four assessments are individual. Ordinary discussion of course material —
what a review session covers, how the six-stage cycle works, what a term in
a brief means — follows your university's normal rules on collaboration
versus misconduct; this page does not restate them. What must be your own
work, regardless of how much discussion preceded it, is the observation,
hypothesis, evidence interpretation, diagnosis, intervention, and
verification you submit. Comparing notes on a system with a classmate and
then submitting their diagnosis as your own reasoning is not collaboration —
it fails the same test as retrofitting a hypothesis after the fact, for the
same reason.

## Tool use

No assessment is graded on proficiency with a particular debugger, logging
framework, or language — the course is explicit that tools are incidental to
a given exercise, not its subject. What's graded is why you chose a
particular observation or tool, what evidence it produced, and how that
evidence changed which hypotheses were still live. A dramatic-looking tool
used without a stated reason earns nothing that a plain `print` statement
used with one wouldn't.

## AI and coding agents

AI tools and coding agents may assist with understanding an unfamiliar
system, searching a codebase, explaining a language feature, or performing a
mechanical code operation. They do not change what you are responsible for:
you must be able to account for every piece of evidence you submit, defend
every hypothesis you claim to have held, explain every intervention you
made, and answer questions about your own reasoning. A conclusion produced by
an AI tool is not itself evidence — if you use one, you must independently
validate whatever it tells you before it enters your investigation trail,
the same way you would treat an unverified claim from any other source.

## Uncertainty

Recording "unknown," "unverified," or "insufficient evidence to conclude" is
an accepted, expected outcome of a genuine investigation, not a gap to be
smoothed over before submission. A claim stated with confidence that
matches the evidence you actually have is worth more than a confident
claim the evidence doesn't support — this applies to every assessment in the
course, and is graded accordingly rather than penalised for honesty.
