Nothing new is being taught this week. Every skill this session draws on —
observation, falsifiable hypothesis, evidence selection, discrimination
between explanations, evidence provenance, intervention scoping, and
causal verification — was already exercised somewhere in Weeks 1 through
11. What's new is that all of it now has to work together, unassisted, and
survive being questioned by someone who wasn't in the room while you did it.

## A trail is a chain, and a chain has weak links

Consider a compressed investigation trail: an observation, a hypothesis it
motivated, evidence gathered to test that hypothesis, a diagnosis drawn from
it, an intervention, and a verification. A useful way to examine any trail
— yours or someone else's — is to ask:

```text
Which transition in this reasoning chain is weakly supported?
```

Not "is the final diagnosis correct," which invites defending the
conclusion, but which specific step — hypothesis from observation, evidence
choice from hypothesis, diagnosis from evidence, verification from
diagnosis — is doing less work than the others assume it did. Every trail
has at least one link that's more assumed than earned; finding it in your
own trail before someone else does is the actual preparation for this week.

## Defence is not performance

A live defence is not a presentation to rehearse until it sounds smooth. A
polished narrative and a defensible trail are different things, and
questioning is designed to tell them apart. Expect questions aimed
directly at the weak links: why a hypothesis was formed the way it was,
what result would have changed your conclusion, why a particular source was
trusted, which alternative was considered and how it was ruled out, what
your verification actually demonstrates, and — just as pointedly — what it
doesn't. There is no advance list of questions, because the questions exist
to probe whatever your specific trail leaves open, not a fixed script.

## Stating uncertainty is not a weaker answer

An investigation that ends with "the cause is most likely X, verified this
way, with Y still unresolved" is not an incomplete answer to a defence
question — for a system with genuine remaining ambiguity, it may be the
only honest one. What a defence penalises is not remaining uncertainty; it's
uncertainty that goes unstated, or a claim asserted with more confidence
than the evidence behind it actually earned.

## What this week prepares you for

The synthesis exercise this week and the Final immediately following it
both ask for the same thing: a system you haven't seen, no framing of what
matters, and a trail that has to hold up under questions you can't
anticipate in advance. Nothing about scale changes that requirement — a
larger repository doesn't make a weak link in your reasoning any less
weak.
